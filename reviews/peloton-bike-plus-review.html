What it does well

  • The swivel screen turns the bike into a base for strength and yoga, which is what makes one membership cover a household's whole training rather than only the cycling.
  • Class based motivation genuinely works for people who do not respond to a training plan, and consistency beats optimisation.
  • The Bike+ is well engineered and quiet, with a belt drive and magnetic resistance that need almost no maintenance.
  • Automatic resistance in classes removes the fiddling that stops people following an interval structure on a manual bike.

Where it falls short

  • Six hundred dollars a year of membership does not stop, and after five years it exceeds the price of the bike.
  • The bike's saddle and fit are not your road bike's fit, so it does not prepare you for a long outdoor ride the way a smart trainer does.
  • Its power figures are its own. They are consistent and they will not match a power meter on your bike, so do not carry an FTP between them.
  • Hardware depends on the company continuing to support it, which is a real risk with any screen based connected machine.

What owners report

Published owner reports are strongly positive about build quality, quietness and the class experience. Households report that one membership covering several people is what makes the cost defensible.

The consistent criticism is the subscription and what the bike becomes without it. Reports from cyclists frequently note that they moved to a smart trainer once they started training for outdoor events, which is a useful signal about who the product is genuinely for.

Who it suits

  • People who need instructors and a class schedule to train consistently.
  • Households where several people will share the membership.
  • Anyone who wants a self contained bike with no setup and no bike of their own.

Who should skip it

  • Cyclists training for outdoor riding, who need their own fit and their own power numbers.
  • Anyone unwilling to pay a subscription indefinitely.
  • Riders who already use Zwift or TrainerRoad, where a trainer or a Keiser costs less and does more.

Common questions

Is the Peloton membership worth it?

It depends entirely on whether the classes are what makes you train. At $49.99 a month it is roughly six hundred dollars a year, and it does not stop. For a household where several people ride, that is defensible. For a solo cyclist following a training plan, it is money spent on content that goes unopened.

Peloton Bike+ or a smart trainer?

A smart trainer puts your own bike, saddle and fit under you and produces power numbers you can carry outdoors. The Bike+ gives classes and needs no bike. If you ride outdoors, the trainer is the training tool. Our Peloton versus Wahoo smart trainers comparison covers it directly.

Does the Bike+ power match my bike's power meter?

No, and you should not expect it to. Every indoor bike calculates or measures power its own way. Keep separate FTP figures for indoors and outdoors rather than trying to reconcile them. Our FTP calculator works from a test on whichever machine you are actually using.

What happens if I stop paying?

Peloton states the membership unlocks the content and applicable features, so the practical answer is that you keep a well built stationary bike and lose most of what you paid for. Factor that into how you value the hardware.

Is it good for cycling fitness?

Yes, for aerobic fitness and leg strength. What it is less good at is preparing you for the specific demands of riding your own bike outdoors: position, handling, and sustained steady efforts rather than class intervals. Our smart trainer versus spin bike comparison covers the difference.
