Stability vs Neutral Running Shoes

Pronation-based shoe fitting has not held up well in research, which changes how this decision should be made.

Verdict

Choose on comfort, not on a pronation reading. The long-standing practice of matching shoes to foot roll has repeatedly failed to show an injury benefit in controlled studies, and the most consistent predictor of both comfort and lower injury rates is simply the shoe that feels best to run in. Try a neutral shoe first, and try a stability shoe if the neutral one feels unstable.

For two decades, running shops assessed pronation and sold shoes accordingly. Several controlled trials since, including large military cohort studies, have failed to find that prescribing shoes by foot type reduces injuries, and some found no difference at all. That is not the same as saying stability shoes are useless; plenty of runners genuinely prefer them. It means the prescription model was doing less than it claimed.

What has held up better is comfort. Studies of shoe comfort and injury have generally found that runners allowed to select the most comfortable option get on better than those assigned a shoe by category.

If you keep getting injured

Recurrent running injuries are far more often a training load problem than a shoe problem. Sudden increases in weekly mileage or intensity, insufficient recovery and a history of previous injury are the factors that show up most consistently in the research. If you are injured repeatedly, a physiotherapist or sports doctor looking at your training and your movement is a better investment than another shoe.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need stability shoes if I overpronate?

Not necessarily. Controlled studies, including large military trials, have generally failed to show that prescribing shoes by foot type reduces injuries. Some degree of pronation is normal and functional. Choose the shoe that feels most comfortable to run in, which is the selection method with better support behind it.

How do I know which shoe is right?

Run in them. Comfort is the most reliable guide, and it is a better predictor than a wet footprint test or a treadmill gait video. Most specialist running shops will let you run in shoes before buying. Fit at the end of the day, with the socks you actually run in, and leave a thumb's width at the toe.

Are motion control shoes still a thing?

The heavily corrective motion control category has largely been replaced by softer approaches such as guide rails and broader midsoles, which nudge rather than block. That shift followed the research failing to support the older, more prescriptive model.

How often should I replace running shoes?

Commonly 300 to 500 miles, though it depends on your weight, your gait, the surface and the shoe. The more useful signals are the midsole feeling flat and dead, new aches appearing on familiar runs, and visible compression creasing along the foam.
